A Seed Law for Farmers, Not Just for Corporations

AfrONews held an interview with Daud Ngosengwa Manongi, a Programs Lead for Biodiversity Conservation at the Tanzania Alliance for Biodiversity (TABIO). He is also a Steering Committee Member of the Global IFOAM Seed Platform, the Secretary General of the Seed Working Group in Tanzania's Civil Society Organizations (CSOs), and an expert representative on the Advisory Committee on Seed Unity (ACSU) under the International Treaty on Plant Genetic Resources for Food and Agriculture (ITPGRFA), to understand the legal framework surrounding Farmer-Managed Seed Systems (FMSS) in Tanzania. Excerpts:

What Is a Farmer-Managed Seed System?

AfrONews: What exactly is a farmer-managed seed system, and why does it matter more to ordinary Tanzanians than the formal seed system that the law currently favors?

Manongi: A farmer-managed seed system is simply the practice where farmers save seeds from their own harvest, exchange them with neighbors, or buy them from local grain markets. This is not a small or marginal practice — in Tanzania, it accounts for more than eighty percent of the total area planted with subsistence crops. Why does this matter? Because these local seed varieties have been developed by farmers over generations. They are resilient to drought, resistant to local pests, and highly nutritious. Studies have shown that ninety-seven percent of maize landraces are resistant to storage pests, and eighty-five percent are drought resistant. Compare that to hybrid seeds, which are expensive, often unavailable in remote areas, and can fail under harsh conditions. The farmer-managed seed system is not backward. It is the backbone of Tanzanian agriculture.

A Law That Criminalizes the Majority

AfrONews: The Seed Act of 2003 prohibits the exchange and sale of farmers' seeds, yet more than 80 percent of Tanzanian smallholder farmers depend on this practice. How do you explain this contradiction between the law and the reality on the ground?

Manongi: The contradiction is stark and deeply problematic. The Seed Act of 2003 was designed primarily for the commercial seed industry, focusing on certified improved varieties, plant breeders' rights, and formal quality control mechanisms. It simply did not consider the reality that the vast majority of Tanzanian farmers would continue to rely on their own saved seeds, local exchanges, and grain market purchases. As a result, the law technically prohibits the very practices that feed the nation — a grandmother saving maize seeds from her harvest for the next season is operating in a legal grey area. The consequence is that farmers are left vulnerable, with no legal protection when their seeds are confiscated, no recognized rights when they want to sell surplus seeds to neighbors, and no recourse when policies designed for commercial agriculture undermine their traditional practices. We cannot have a situation where the majority of farmers are technically lawbreakers simply for feeding their families.

What NEOAS Changed, and What Still Needs to Change

AfrONews: The National Ecological Organic Agriculture Strategy (NEOAS) 2023-2030 now recognizes farmer-managed seed systems for the first time. What practical difference has this recognition made, and what still needs to change?

Manongi: The adoption of NEOAS 2023-2030 was a significant breakthrough. For the first time, a government document explicitly recognizes farmer-managed seed systems and acknowledges their importance for food security, biodiversity conservation, and climate resilience. This recognition did not happen by accident — it was the result of twenty years of relentless advocacy by TABIO, the Tanzania Organic Agriculture Movement (TOAM), and our partners. However, recognition in a strategy document is not the same as legal protection. The Seed Act of 2003 remains unchanged. What we have seen are pilot initiatives — the Tanzania Official Seed Certification Institute (TOSCI) has officially registered twelve farmer varieties. Twelve varieties out of thousands that farmers depend on. That is a start, but it is not enough. We need a separate legal instrument dedicated to farmers' seeds. NEOAS opened the door. Now we need to walk through it.

Are Farmer-Managed Seeds Unpredictable?

AfrONews: Some argue that formal seed systems guarantee quality, yield, and food safety, while farmer-managed seeds are unpredictable. How do you respond to that concern?

Manongi: I understand that concern, and it is a fair one. There is some truth to it — formal certification does provide a level of assurance that informal systems cannot match. But here is what the critics often miss: farmer-managed seeds have been tested not in laboratories, but in the real world over generations. A maize variety that has survived fifty seasons in a drought-prone village has proven its resilience in ways that no laboratory trial can replicate. The two systems are not enemies — they can coexist. What we are advocating for is a pluralistic seed system where farmers have genuine choice. Farmers are intelligent people. They are best placed to make these decisions for themselves, their families, and their land.

One Change That Would Matter Most

AfrONews: If TABIO and its partners could change just one thing in Tanzania's seed policy tomorrow, what would that one thing be, and why?

Manongi: It would be this: create a separate legal framework specifically for farmer-managed seed systems that recognizes the right of farmers to save, use, exchange, and sell their own seeds without requiring certification or registration. I would not touch the Seed Act of 2003 — it serves the commercial seed industry well, and it has its place. But the commercial system serves only a small fraction of Tanzanian farmers. The majority operate outside it. They need their own law, one that fits their reality. This single change would do more than any other to protect Tanzania's agricultural biodiversity, strengthen food security, and respect the rights of millions of smallholder farmers who have been feeding this nation for generations. The law should work for farmers, not against them. That is what we are fighting for.
