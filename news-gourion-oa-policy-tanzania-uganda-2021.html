A Visitor's Perspective on OA Policy Formulation Progress in Tanzania and Uganda

Ms Mathilde Gourion, agricultural policies trainee from the French agricultural research and cooperation organization CIRAD, recently completed a study on agricultural policy in Tanzania and Uganda under the Institutional Innovation for Organic Agriculture in Africa (IIABA) project. She spoke to AfrONet staff writer Constantine Akitanda about her findings.

On Tanzania’s progress, Gourion said her trip aligned with current efforts to mainstream organic agriculture in the National Agriculture Policy (NAP) and develop a separate organic agriculture strategy under it. The NAP has been under revision since 2019; organic stakeholders have already submitted comments and are waiting to see if these were included, with a consultant currently working on an evaluation report for the Cabinet Secretariat. Organic stakeholders have obtained authorization from the Ministry of Agriculture to develop an OA strategy as a key part of the NAP, with formulation starting in July 2021, potentially supported by IIABA.

Asked about the policy component’s weight within the project, Gourion said 23 respondents in mainland Tanzania identified the main challenges facing the organic sector: production-related issues (30 percent of cited challenges), followed by institutional framework (26 percent), including lack of government support, lack of extension officer knowledge, lack of policy support, government support for chemical farming, and the NAP not being operationalized through a programme.

Lessons for African Policy

Asked what lessons she drew for African countries generally, Gourion said there is growing interest in the organic sector in both Uganda and Tanzania — shown in the adoption of the National Organic Agriculture Policy (NOAP) in Uganda and inclusion of policy statements for OA in Tanzania’s NAP — but that the biggest lesson is to account for each country’s specific legal and policy framework: Ugandan stakeholders said the NOAP needs operationalizing through proper legislation, while in Tanzania operationalization depended more on institutional relationships and coordination.

She also noted that organic efforts are undermined by better-funded chemical farming programmes, and called for more coordination among institutional stakeholders, and for the development of a strong, well-organized organic sector able to sell products in national, regional and international markets, rather than relying on organic policy alone. “My main message is that there is more to organic agriculture than a low-production, outdated way of farming. Organic agriculture needs resources and research to be conducted to reach its full potential in terms of productivity,” she said. Asked to define Africa based on her time in Tanzania and Uganda, and five years living across the continent, Gourion said she could not: “Africa has so many cultures, languages, places, practices... I would not be able to define it.”
