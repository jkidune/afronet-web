UN Report: 34% of Agricultural Land Is Degraded, Water Overdrawn

The land and water resources farmers rely on are today stressed to “a breaking point”, even as almost 10 percent of the eight billion people on earth are already undernourished, with three billion lacking healthy diets, warns a new report from the United Nations Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO). By 2050 there will be two billion more mouths to feed. For now, farmers have boosted productivity by irrigating more land and applying heavier doses of fertilizer and pesticides — but the report says these practices are not sustainable: they have eroded and degraded soil while polluting and depleting water supplies and shrinking the world’s forests.

Globally, agriculture accounts for 72 percent of all surface and groundwater withdrawals, mainly for irrigation, depleting groundwater aquifers in many regions; global groundwater withdrawals for irrigated agriculture increased by about 20 percent over the past decade alone. The quality of 13 percent of global soil, including 34 percent of agricultural land, has been degraded by factors such as excessive fertilizer use, livestock overgrazing, deforestation and decreasing water availability. “Human-induced degradation affects 34 percent — 1,660 million hectares — of agricultural land,” the report says.

Deforestation offers one relatively bright spot: the global forested area declined by about one percent, or 47 million hectares, over the past decade — a significant improvement from the nearly two percent decline of the 1990s. At the November 2021 climate negotiations in Glasgow, 141 countries covering 91 percent of global forested area agreed to halt and reverse forest loss and land degradation by 2030, though it remains to be seen how many will meet those commitments.

Four Action Areas

The FAO report recommends four action areas: adopting inclusive land and water governance through improved land-use planning; implementing integrated solutions at scale, such as helping farmers use resources more efficiently while building climate resilience; embracing innovative technologies like remote sensing and improved rainwater capture; and investing in long-term sustainable land, soil and water management, including restoring degraded ecosystems. Sustainable practices like regenerative agriculture can also double as climate solutions, requiring less diesel-fuelled machinery and pesticide reliance while increasing carbon stored in farmed soils — FAO reports 31 percent of global greenhouse gas emissions come from agri-food systems. What is needed now, the report concludes, is for policymakers and planners to coordinate with farmers to adopt more sustainable practices and adapt more quickly to the changing climate.
