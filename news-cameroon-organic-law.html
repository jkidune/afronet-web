Cameroon's New Organic Law Sets the Stage, but Will Small Farmers Be Left Behind?

For years, organic farming in Cameroon lived in the shadows, stripped of rules, denied recognition, sustained only by farmers who did the right thing simply because they believed. They grew naturally, as their grandparents had done for generations, while exporters struggled for credibility and consumers remained unable to tell what was truly organic from what was simply a label.

Then came 17 December 2025. On that day, President Paul Biya signed Law No. 2025/013 governing organic agriculture. After decades of informal practice, Cameroon finally has a legal framework.

The new legislation sets clear rules for production, certification, labeling, and traceability, covering crops, livestock, aquaculture, beekeeping, and even processed products. Genetically modified organisms are out, excessive synthetic chemicals are banned, and for the first time, anyone caught falsely claiming their product is organic can face real penalties.

This law closes many loopholes; it creates a state-supervised certification system, and for the first time, Cameroonian organic producers can point to a legal standard, not just good intentions and hope. The global organic market is worth about $136 billion — roughly FCFA 89 trillion — without a legal framework, Cameroon could not compete: exporters saw shipments rejected, and international buyers asked for proof that simply did not exist. “The law is not just about regulation,” an agriculture ministry official told us, speaking on condition of anonymity. “It is about unlocking markets.”

A Longtime Advocate's Caution

Josephine Atangana, AfrONet's Treasurer, knows this struggle better than most — a longtime advocate for organic farming and founder of a women-led cooperative in the Centre Region, she has spent years fighting for recognition, not for herself, but for the women she trains. Since 2018, she has trained more than 500 women in agroecological practices, watching them struggle, save, and slowly build better lives; Atangana sees both promise and caution in the new law.

“For many years, we have farmed organically without recognition,” she said, her voice steady but firm. “This law finally gives us an identity, however the real work begins now — training our farmers, building affordable certification, and ensuring that smallholders are not left behind. If we get this right, organic agriculture can lift thousands of families out of poverty,” she hinted. “A law on paper means nothing if the farmer in the field cannot afford the certificate,” she added quietly. You could hear the weight of experience in those words.

Building a National Certification Framework

In February 2026, INADES-Formation Cameroon brought everyone together in Yaoundé, with a goal to strengthen the Cameroon Organic Agriculture Movement as a national certification framework. That movement is now exploring low-cost participatory guarantee systems — a simpler, cheaper way for smallholders to get certified without breaking the bank.

Three things need to happen, and quickly: first, the implementing decrees must be published; second, affordable certification pathways must be established; and third, farmers need real training to meet the new standards. Cameroon has taken the hardest step, enshrining organic agriculture into law, a feat worth celebrating, and yet the coming year will reveal whether this legislation transforms the lives of the farmers who actually feed the nation or merely remains a beautiful document that never touches the soil.
