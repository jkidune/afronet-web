The Farmer Who Had Refused to Give Up

The sun is brutal over Manica province. It beats down on red earth and tired backs, but John Moteriua doesn't mind. He's too busy inspecting his crops, checking leaves that no longer bear the scars of chemical burns, feeling the soil that has grown darker and richer under his care.

His hands, once stained by synthetic pesticides, now work with compost and natural enrichers. The results, he says, speak for themselves. “These new practices reduce spending and increase profits,” he says, wiping sweat from his brow.

Moteriua is not alone. Across Mozambique, a quiet agricultural revolution is unfolding, one rooted not in costly chemical inputs, but in the wisdom of organic farming and agroecology. From the flood-ravaged fields of Gaza to the rural cooperatives of Niassa, smallholder farmers are reclaiming their land and their livelihoods.

A Homemade Pesticide

In Manica, farmers have learned to create their own pesticide using leaves from the Muquina plant, combined with ordinary bar soap, hot pepper, and tobacco. They soak the mixture for a day, drain the liquid, and dilute it with water — it's simple, it's cheap, and it works. Artur Tunguza, president of a local farmers' club in Vanduzi district, explains: “We combine all these ingredients, adding 200 grams with a one-inch size piece of soap to create a strong pesticide that helps combat resistant pests.”

Building Capacity From the Ground Up

The movement is spreading in Niassa province, under the SABIO project run by We Effect Mozambique in partnership with local farmers' unions and backed by the Liechtenstein Development Service. “The core of SABIO is about building capacity from the ground up, starting with the least reached communities,” says Aires Afonso Mbanze, the project manager. Omar Salimo, president of one revitalised farmers' association, remembers the old days: “Before SABIO, very few farmers grew vegetables collectively. Now, we produce together, especially for selling, and many farmers have replaced chemical inputs with organic fertilisers.”

Ten Kilograms of Tomatoes

At Niassa's agricultural institutes, instructors are training students to use effective microorganisms, a type of biofertiliser mixed with organic waste, and the results have stunned even the most skeptical. Almiro Hugo Cardoso, an agricultural technician, can barely contain his excitement: “We're harvesting up to 10kg of tomatoes per plant, compared to an average of 5kg that we used to produce in our demo plots.”

Mozambique's government is beginning to pay attention: in April 2025, the Ministry of Agriculture, Environment and Fisheries was established with a mandate to promote sustainable development, a significant step, though formal recognition is still a work in progress.

Alberto Ndala, a project officer from the Union of Farmers and Associations of Niassa, is candid about the difficulties. “Combating pests with botanical pesticides can be expensive, sometimes more costly than the additional yield itself,” he admits. Through initiatives like SUSTAIN, supported by the Norwegian and Swedish development agencies, over 1,200 farmers have gained skills in climate-smart agriculture, agroforestry, water conservation and biodiversity protection.

But for the farmers themselves, the shift is about more than yields or profits; it is about something deeper. Chumine Saide, a farmer in Niassa, puts it simply: “It's not just about money, instead about knowing that we can manage on our own.” In a country where so much has been taken, that sense of independence is priceless. Mozambique's organic movement is not a luxury; it is a lifeline.
