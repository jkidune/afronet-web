Why Is Morocco Winning the Organic Race While the Rest of Africa Lags Behind?

Imagine a country that offers farmers up to 90 percent off their certification costs, pours nearly $150 million into organic agriculture, and has a roadmap to convert 100,000 hectares to organic farming by 2030. That country is not in Europe, not in North America — it is Morocco, and it is showing the rest of Africa exactly how to build a resilient, drought-proof agricultural future.

While most African nations continue subsidizing chemical fertilizers and monoculture exports, Morocco has quietly constructed one of the continent's most sophisticated organic agriculture support systems, moving beyond pilot projects and declarations to implement a comprehensive strategy that integrates financial incentives, institutional coordination, and grassroots empowerment.

A National Priority, Not an Afterthought

Morocco's commitment to organic farming is embedded in the nation's core agricultural strategy. Under the “Generation Green 2020-2030” plan, the government has set an ambitious target: expand organic farmland to 100,000 hectares by 2030, backed by a contract-program worth nearly 1.5 billion dirhams (approximately $150 million) with the organic sector's umbrella organisation, Maroc Bio. The state and the private sector are contributing equally — a partnership model that ensures shared responsibility and shared success.

Financial Support That Actually Works

Morocco understands that farmers will not transition to organic practices unless it makes economic sense. The government has introduced subsidies that cover up to 90 per cent of certification costs for smaller production units — certification being one of the biggest barriers to organic farming across Africa. The government also provides hectare-based conversion premiums and maintains support for investment in processing units. This is not charity; it is smart economics that builds a self-sustaining sector.

Building the Institutional Framework

Morocco has systematically built the institutional architecture needed for organic agriculture to thrive: the National Institute for Agronomic Research (INRA) conducts research, the National Food Safety Office (ONSSA) oversees certification and control, and the Agricultural Development Agency (ADA) coordinates support programs. Through the DIAF project, a German-Moroccan technical dialogue has further strengthened Morocco's capacity to build legal, institutional, and market frameworks — the goal is not just production but a complete value chain.

Beyond Policy

Morocco's organic revolution is also about people, especially women and youth. The 2023-2030 contract-programs prioritize young entrepreneurs, rural women, and agricultural organizations. Recent agreements signed with NGO consortiums in the Fès-Meknès, Oriental, and Souss-Massa regions aim to convert 8,000 hectares to agroecological practices while supporting 2,700 family farms. This is where Morocco gets it right: policy is not imposed from above; it is co-created with communities on the ground.

A Lesson for Africa

In 2018, certified organic farmland had more than doubled in five years to over 9,300 hectares, with wild collection areas covering another 273,000 hectares. Organic production surged from 40,000 tons in 2010 to 120,000 tons in 2019, and Moroccan organic products — citrus, olives, and tomatoes — now reach markets in the United States, Germany, France, Spain, and Italy.

As the continent faces a drought crisis that, by 2050, will leave three out of every four people facing water scarcity, the question is: will other African nations follow Morocco's lead, or will they watch from the sidelines as the crisis deepens? “Africa has the land, the people and the potential. What we need now is unity, strategy and bold action,” says Youssef Elharrak, Organic Exporter & Policy Advisor, Rabat, Morocco.
