Introducing the French Connection to Agroecology

The government of France is providing significant support to mitigate the changing climate of agricultural production models, in order to combine economic, social and environmental requirements, including the growing importance of organic agriculture. Constantine Akitanda held an interview with the French ambassador to Tanzania, Hon. Frédéric Clavier.

Asked why the French government supports agroecology, the ambassador said France has a longer-term vision for promoting climate change adaptation and mitigation, as opposed to intensive agriculture, which has a short-term vision. Most food production is based on farming systems that require a lot of natural resources — a model that is not sustainable, is harmful to the environment, and increases degradation of soils, forests, water, air quality and biodiversity. “Intensive agriculture favours short-term yields ahead of respecting and preserving natural resources. This model of production cannot eradicate hunger,” he said.

On why agroecology offers a long-term answer to climate change, the ambassador said it promotes adaptation and mitigation, helping protect natural resources, increase biodiversity, restore productivity, improve soil fertility, sequester carbon and regulate climate — improving the resilience of rural communities to climate hazards, especially in countries like Tanzania where erratic rainfall could jeopardize food security. Studies also show that organic methods used by smallholder farmers can increase food security among the poor, with crop diversification helping provide a healthier, more balanced diet and tackling hidden hunger and micronutrient deficiencies.

On opportunities for farmers to increase incomes, the ambassador pointed to a great opportunity for smallholder farmers in Tanzania and across Africa, given huge demand for organic products in local economies and in international markets including the USA, Europe and India. In several regions of Tanzania the soils are not contaminated by pesticides and could easily be converted to organic farming — unlike in France. Today, half a million farmers in cooperatives in Tanzania are already certified organic and accessing organic and fair-trade markets.

France currently supports two agroecology projects in Tanzania: a 1.48 million euro grant from the French Development Agency to AfrONet to support institutional innovation for organic agriculture across Tanzania, Uganda and Morocco (the IIABA project), and a separate one-million-euro project over 2019-2020 co-financed with Swissaid and Tanzanian NGOs including Sustainable Agriculture Tanzania (SAT), the Tanzania Organic Agriculture Movement (TOAM) and the Tanzania Alliance for Biodiversity (TABIO), aimed at improving sustainable rural livelihoods while protecting the environment and biodiversity.
