From ISAN to Mzizi: Rooting Agroecology Storytelling in African Soil

When ISAN Magazine became Mzizi Magazine in 2026, the change raised more than a few eyebrows across Southern Africa's agroecology community. Why fix something that wasn't broken? To find out, Constantine Akitanda sat down with Fortunate Nyakanda, Co-Editor of Mzizi Magazine, to understand the thinking behind the rebrand, and what it means for the grassroots CSOs, farmers and practitioners who rely on the platform as a vital source of knowledge, connection and advocacy. Excerpts:

A Philosophical Shift

Question: The shift from the regional identifier ISAN to Mzizi (root) is a philosophical one. What key strategic discussions led the team to prioritise a name reflecting diverse knowledge systems over a purely geographic scope?

Answer: The shift from ISAN to Mzizi emerged from strategic reflections on the magazine's evolving role. While ISAN reflected our origins within a regional network supported by the Knowledge Hub for Organic Agriculture and Agroecology in Southern Africa, co-founder Stefanie Swanepoel and I recognised that the stories we document — rooted in farmer practice, indigenous knowledge and community experience — extend beyond geographic boundaries. Mzizi, a Swahili word meaning root, was deliberately chosen in its plural form to acknowledge the many knowledge systems, cultures and relationships that sustain African food systems.

Sourcing Knowledge From the Ground Up

Question: How does the Mzizi rebrand practically change the way you source and validate farmer-led and indigenous knowledge? Will this make it easier for grassroots CSOs with limited resources to contribute to and utilise the platform?

Answer: The Mzizi rebrand does not change how we source and validate knowledge — it deepens our commitment to it. We now source stories more deliberately through civil society networks, farmer organisations and community practitioners on the ground. Validation blends farmer testimony, practitioner experience and relevant research — ensuring credibility without stripping away authenticity. Our partnership model offers editorial support and narrative mentoring, allowing organisations without communications budgets to contribute; we help shape raw field experience into publishable stories and amplify them across networks.

Scaling Agroecology Continent-Wide

Question: Your vision now spans “across Africa.” How does this rebranding strategically position Mzizi to facilitate the cross-pollination of agroecological practices between Southern Africa and other regions, helping to scale the movement continent-wide?

Answer: The name Mzizi does more than signal roots. It opens doors. By shedding a purely regional identifier, the magazine now creates space for cross-continental dialogue. Stories, innovations and lessons from Southern Africa can travel north, and those from West, East and Central Africa can travel south. Mzizi facilitates this exchange through thematic editions and knowledge partnerships, translating research, programme experiences and grassroots innovations into accessible narratives that scale agroecology, from the roots up, across the continent.

A Cooperative, Not a Publisher

Question: How does operating under the Mycelium Media Colab Cooperative change the partnership model for CSOs? Does this structure offer more collaborative ownership and flexibility compared to a traditional publishing model?

Answer: Mzizi is not published by a traditional media house — it is published by a cooperative. Mycelium Media Colab brings together more than 60 regenerative storytellers based across Africa, spanning videography, podcasting, participatory theatre and mobile journalism. Any organisation can submit stories for editorial consideration at no cost; for those seeking deeper collaboration, Mzizi offers story packages that directly employ our network of 42 regional journalists and 60 cooperative members, with ten percent of every payment flowing back into the cooperative to train youth in multimedia storytelling. This is not a publisher-client relationship. It is a shared ecosystem, a model built on ownership, not extraction.
